Scene 1 of 5 · The prediction

How big can each learning step be before training blows up?

The page in 30 seconds

  1. Too big a step overshoots. Like a shower tap turned too far, each correction makes things worse. Try the shower.
  2. Randomness lowers the safe step. Learning from a few random examples at a time shakes the program, the way a child pumping a swing feeds it energy. Scene 3.
  3. Links lower it much more. Knobs that are safe one by one can fail together, like walkers on a swaying bridge: here at a step about 144 times smaller. Scene 4.

When a computer "learns" from data, as AI models do, it turns thousands of little knobs (its internal numbers) a bit at a time, each turn chosen to make its mistakes smaller. How far it turns them each time is the step size, written η (the Greek letter eta). Small turns learn slowly. Turns that are too big overshoot: the mistakes grow instead of shrinking, and training blows up. The boundary between the two is the limit η* ("eta-star").

Everyday versionThink of setting the hot water in a shower. Small turns of the tap get you there slowly. Big turns overshoot: too hot, so you turn back hard, too cold, then hotter still, and you never settle. Past a certain size of turn, every correction overshoots more than the one before. A learning program is the same, and one equation gives exactly where that size is.

  • One equationOne equation says exactly where the limit η* is, for a simplified but standard kind of learning program. It includes two common habits of real training: momentum (keeping part of the last move) and learning from a few random examples at a time (Theorem 1(a) of the note; who found which part first is in the footer).
  • Up to 144.5 times smallerthan what you would get by checking each knob on its own, in the example of scene 4.
  • How fast it settlesBelow the limit the error settles at a speed that follows from the same equation, and that speed drops to zero at the limit. For bell-curve examples the speed was found before; the note proves it for more kinds of examples and adds how it falls to zero (scene 5).

In the chart, 64 copies of the same small learning program train at once: 32 with a step size 10 % below the predicted limit (blue) and 32 with one 10 % above it (red). Change the settings: the prediction moves, and the runs follow it.

What is proved

The program learns by repeating one simple rule: new knob settings = old settings + a share of the last change (momentum) − a correction worked out from a few random examples, scaled by the step size.

The average error stays under control exactly when the step size is below η*, and one equation gives η*. For the bell-curve examples used here, that equation was already known (Liu, Ziyin and Ueda, 2021; Ziyin et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2026). The note extends it to examples whose shape differs from knob to knob, and proves how fast the error settles below the limit. The page solves it for you and prints the answer under the buttons. The formulas are in the technical version.

Why it happens

A bending steel bar and a learning program follow the same curve

Below the limit the mistakes settle down, but the closer the step size gets to the limit, the higher they settle; near the limit they shoot up. Engineers know this exact curve from columns. Below, a steel bar and a learning program side by side.

Everyday versionHold a plastic ruler by its two ends and push them toward each other. At first it hardly bends. Push harder and it bows more, then much more, and near a certain force it suddenly gives way to the side. Engineers call that force the buckling load. A learning program near its limit behaves the same way: little change far from it, then a sudden rise.

Words on the learning side

Training
The computer turns its knobs (its internal numbers) over and over, to make fewer mistakes on examples.
Step size η
How far each turn of the knobs goes. Also called the learning rate.
Batch, B
Each turn is decided from B = 4 examples picked at random (the setting chosen above). Like asking four random people for directions: usually about right, sometimes off.
Momentum β
Each turn keeps a share β of the previous one, like a shopping cart that keeps rolling after you push it.
Error ℓ
How far the program is from the best possible knob settings, averaged over many runs.
Limit η*
The largest step size at which the average error stays under control. The equation of scene 1 gives it.

Why the bar warns you

The bend grows in a predictable way: at half the breaking load the extra bend equals the starting bend, and at 90 % it is nine times bigger. Because of that, engineers can draw the readings on a special plot where they fall on a straight line, and read the breaking load from its slope long before the bar breaks. That is what the two plots above do.

The same warning in training

The learning program's error follows the same kind of curve: below the limit it settles, at a level that shoots up near the limit. For the current settings the limit is η* = —. If the examples carried no randomness the limit would be —, — times larger: the randomness of the examples is what pulls it down.

Where the comparison stops

The bar settles into a new shape after each push. A learning program never settles completely: every step uses new random examples, so its knobs keep jiggling, and above the limit it is the average size of that jiggling that grows without end. Scene 3 is about exactly that.

Scene 2 of 5 · Steps too big

Without randomness, training moves like a weight on a spring, computed in steps

If the step is small enough, the swings die out. Past a fixed limit, they grow at every step.

Everyday versionVideo games move objects in small time steps. If a game stalls and then catches up with one huge step, objects can fly through walls or shoot off into the sky: the step was too big for how fast things were moving. Here the same thing happens to the knob of a learning program when its step is too big for how sensitive it is.

Take one knob and remove the randomness. Each update then moves it like a weight on a spring: it keeps some of its last move and is pulled back toward the best setting. If the step is too big for how sensitive the knob is, every swing overshoots more than the last. The two pictures below show the same numbers.

How this works: the limit without randomness

Each step multiplies the size of the swing by a number, shown next to the slider. Below 1 the swings shrink; above 1 they grow. That number reaches 1 exactly at the dashed mark: 2 without momentum, 3.8 with momentum 0.9.

A real program has many knobs. Without randomness, the most sensitive knob sets the limit. Scenes 3 and 4 show why randomness can make the true limit much smaller.

Scene 3 of 5 · Random wobble

A stiffness that changes at every step lowers the limit

With random pulls, the limit drops well below the calm one. Above it the average swing grows without end, even while many cables still look calm.

Everyday versionA child on a swing can make it go higher with nobody pushing, just by standing up and crouching down: changing the swing's shape while it moves feeds it energy. Random batches change how sensitive the knob is at every step. They do it at random moments, not at the right ones, but on average they still feed energy into the swing, so it goes wrong earlier than in calm air.

With random examples, how sensitive the knob is changes at every step. On the bridge below, the left half is closed to traffic, so its cables stay calm. On the right half, traffic pulls on the cables at random at every step. Each cable is one run of a one-knob learning program.

How this works: why the limit is lower

With random pulls, the limit is lower than in calm conditions, and momentum makes the effect stronger: at momentum 0.9 the randomness counts 19 times more. With momentum 0.9 and 4 examples per step, the calm half is safe up to — and the traffic half only up to —.

Everyday versionIf 199 people in a room earn an ordinary salary and one is a billionaire, the average salary in the room is enormous, although almost nobody is rich. Above the limit, the average swing explodes the same way: most cables look calm, and a few enormous swings drive the average up.

How many cables snap above the limit depends on the settings. At momentum 0.9 with 4 examples per step only about 5 to 20 of the 200 snap, yet the exact average keeps growing. With less momentum or more examples per step, many more snap.

Scene 4 of 5 · Parts tied together

Each knob is safe on its own; tied together, they fail at a step 144.5 times smaller

With ±1 examples, each knob on its own is safe up to a step size of 3.8. Tied together through the random examples, the knobs fail above 0.0263.

Everyday versionWhen London's Millennium Bridge opened in 2000, it began to sway sideways under the crowd and had to close. Each walker alone was harmless, but the moving bridge pulled people into step, and together they pushed it too hard. Here each knob alone is safe up to a step of 3.8, but the random examples link all 256 knobs, and together they fail at a step about 144 times smaller.

Now the program has 256 knobs, and the examples are made of +1s and −1s. Then each knob, looked at alone, sees no randomness at all and is safe up to a step of 3.8, as in scene 2. But the random examples also link the knobs: turning one changes the error the others feel, at random.

Scene 5 of 5 · How fast it settles

Below the limit the error settles, and more slowly the closer the step is to the limit

At every step the error closes the same share of the gap that is left. That share follows from the same equation as the limit, and it shrinks to nothing at the limit.

Everyday versionPush a plastic ruler from both ends and flick it. With a light push it wobbles back quickly. The harder you push, the more slowly it wobbles, and near the buckling force it barely springs back at all. Engineers use this to estimate the buckling force without breaking anything. A learning program does the same: the closer the step size is to the limit, the longer the error takes to settle.

The program of scene 1 again (momentum 0.9, 4 examples per step). 128 copies start learning from scratch, and each has a twin that has been learning for a while. Both see exactly the same random examples, so the difference between them shows how much of the gap is still left.

How this works

The share comes from the same formula that gives the limit: at the limit the share closed per step is exactly zero, and just below it the share grows in proportion to the distance from the limit. For bell-curve examples this settling speed was found before (Velikanov, Kuznedelev and Yarotsky, 2023). The note proves it for examples whose shape differs from knob to knob, and adds two things: the exact size of the straight line in the left chart, and the slope at which the speed falls to zero at the limit.

The gauge readings carry a small, fixed random error (±0.006 mm), so a given bar always gives the same readings; the learning runs use fixed random seeds too. Theorem and table numbers refer to the note Minibatch Heavy-Ball SGD at Finite Dimension: Exact Threshold, Rate and Critical Slowing Down. Its own parts: examples whose shape differs from knob to knob, a proof of the settling speed for a finite number of knobs, the exact size of the settling line below the limit, the slope at which the speed falls to zero, and how close a finite problem is to the many-knob limit.

Earlier work: for bell-curve (Gaussian) examples the equation for the limit is in Liu, Ziyin and Ueda (ICML 2021) and Ziyin, Liu, Mori and Ueda (ICLR 2022), the step-by-step error equation and the limit in Wang et al. (2026), and the settling speed in Velikanov, Kuznedelev and Yarotsky (ICLR 2023); for examples that are switched on and off at random, with all knobs alike, the step-by-step error equation and the limit are in Everett and Paquette (2026); without momentum and for bell-curve examples the exact error is in Bordelon and Pehlevan (ICLR 2022); early versions for adaptive filters are Feuer and Weinstein (1985) and Roy and Shynk (1990); the limit for any kind of random examples is Theorem F.1 of Cai et al. (2026); that checking one knob at a time gives only a necessary condition is Proposition 9 of Mulayoff and Michaeli (COLT 2024).
