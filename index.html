Scene 1 of 6 · The theorem, applied

The step size where minibatch momentum SGD becomes mean-square unstable

For readers of Everett and Paquette (2026)

  • In their vocabulary model, when the most and the least frequent rows are stable, the slowest row is one of them, at every finite batch (Theorem 2 of the note).
  • No step size and momentum make a gated row decay faster per step than the best SGD step, so Emom = ESGD (Theorem 3, Corollary 4).
  • Tuned momentum is zero for B − 1 ≤ B× and positive for B − 1 > B×, and Smom(B) − Smom = CmomB−1/2 + O(1/B) (Corollary 5, Theorem 6).

Terms: threshold η* · step size η

Theorem 1(a) of the note

Heavy-ball SGD on a quadratic with random minibatch Hessians HBt (mean H) and gradient noise ξt:

wt+1 = ((1 + β)I − ηHBt) wt − β wt−1 − η ξt

When each minibatch averages B random features z = H1/2ω with independent coordinates of mean 0, variance 1 and fourth moment 3 + κ, the second moment E‖wt‖² stays bounded exactly when η is below η*, the smallest positive root (below the first pole) of

S(η) = (ηp / (2(1 − β))) · Σk λk / (1 − ηλkα / (2(1 + β))) = 1

Here λk are the eigenvalues of H, p = 1/B and α = 1 + p(1 + κ)(1 + β)/(1 − β). For η ≥ η* the second moment grows at least linearly in t (Lemma 1(iii) of the longer note in the repository, with gradient noise Σξ ≻ 0). For arbitrary per-sample Hessians, η* is the reciprocal of the top eigenvalue of an explicit operator (Theorem F.1 of Cai et al., 2026). For Gaussian features (κ = 0, the case run on this page) this equation and the steady loss f/(1 − S(η)) are in Liu, Ziyin and Ueda (2021) and Ziyin et al. (2022, Theorem 2), and Wang et al. (2026, Appendix B) obtain the threshold from a renewal equation; for isotropic gated features the threshold and the renewal equation are in Everett and Paquette (2026). The note's own part here is the model: mode-dependent kurtosis κk and a random scale (scene 4 uses it with Rademacher features).

Why it happens

A column and a network with the same amplification curve

Below η* the average loss settles at f/(1 − S(η)) (Theorem 1(a)), and the factor 1/(1 − S(η)) grows without bound as S(η) approaches 1. A pinned steel bar has the same factor: with a small initial bow a₀ and a load P, it bows by a₀/(1 − P/Pcr). Below, the two systems side by side.

One curve for both

Column: the total bow at mid-height is a₀ / (1 − P/Pcr), so x = P/Pcr. Network: once training settles, the average loss is f / (1 − S(η)), where S(η) is the left-hand side of the secular equation and f stays finite at the limit (Theorem 1(a)), so x = S(η). The height of the network point is measured: the average loss of the 48 runs divided by f. Its position x = S(η) is computed from the formula. Above η* there is no steady level, and the point is drawn at the limit. In a real lab you would not know Pcr; this page knows it so that you can check the method below.

How far each update moves the weights along the negative gradient (the learning rate).
Minibatch, B
Each update uses B = 4 random examples (the batch size chosen above), so both the gradient and the curvature it sees are noisy.
Momentum β
Each update keeps a fraction β of the previous one, like a mass that carries inertia.
Loss ℓ
Here ℓt = tr(H E[wt wt⊤]), twice the expected excess loss, where wt is the distance of the weights from the best fit.
Threshold η*
The step size above which ℓ grows without bound (mean-square instability). In this model it is the smallest positive root of the secular equation, Theorem 1(a) of the note.

Why the column warns

Beam theory gives the extra sideways deflection that the gauge reads at mid-height of a pinned bar with initial bow a₀:

δ = a₀ · (P/Pcr) / (1 − P/Pcr)

At half the critical load the extra deflection equals the initial bow. At 90 % it is nine times larger. At the critical load the denominator vanishes and the bar buckles. Euler's formula Pcr = π²EI/L² gives Pcr for an ideal bar, but a 0.1 mm error in a 6 mm thickness changes I by 5 %, and real pins are never ideal. So engineers prefer to measure the bar they have.

Southwell divided the same relation by P:

δ/P = δ/Pcr + a₀/Pcr

This is a straight line in the plane (δ, δ/P). Its slope is 1/Pcr and its intercept gives a₀. Readings between 50 % and 75 % of the critical load are enough, and the bar survives the test. The method is still used for columns, plates and shells.

The same warning in training

The network side runs heavy-ball SGD on a quadratic loss with random minibatch Hessians,

wt+1 = ((1 + β)I − ηHBt) wt − β wt−1 − η ξt

with H = diag(1, 1/2, …, 1/32), Gaussian features, β = 0.9, B = 4 and unit label noise. For this random-feature model the expected loss, started from w = 0, obeys an exact renewal equation ℓt = ft + Σs<t Kt−1−s ℓs (Theorem 1(b) of the note; Wang et al. (2026) write it for Gaussian features). Its kernel sums to

S(η) = (ηp / (2(1 − β))) · Σk λk / (1 − ηλkα / (2(1 + β))),   α = 1 + p(1 + β)(1 + κ)/(1 − β)

with p = 1/B and κ = 0 for Gaussian features. η* is the smallest positive root of S(η) = 1, the one below the first pole (Theorem 1(a)). Below η* the equation gives the steady loss f/(1 − S(η)); above η* there is none. For these settings η* = —. Without curvature noise (every minibatch seeing the Hessian H itself) the limit would be 2(1 + β)/λ₁ = —, — times larger; the gap is the effect of the random curvature.

Where the analogy stops

The column is a static problem: the load rises slowly and the bar finds a new equilibrium at each step. Training is dynamic and random. The weights never stop moving, and what grows without bound is the mean square of their fluctuations. Scene 3 draws a new stiffness at every step.

S(η) is not proportional to η in the way P/Pcr is proportional to P, so a Southwell line drawn in η is only an approximation near the limit.

The six scenes

  • Scene 1The theorem applied, and why training behaves like a buckling column. Above.
  • Scene 2The time step. The mass, spring and damper that an engineer integrates in time. With too large a time step the computation blows up; that is the noiseless limit 2(1 + β)/λ.
  • Scene 3The shaking cable. A stiffness that varies at random makes a structure unstable below the limit computed without noise. This is the minibatch effect on one direction.
  • Scene 4Coupled modes. Each direction alone is stable, yet the coupling lowers the limit. With Rademacher features, P = 256, β = 0.9 and B = 4, the limit is 144.5 times smaller than the one-direction limit.
  • Scene 5The settling rate. Below η* the loss approaches its steady level at a rate predicted from the same kernel, and that rate falls to zero at η*, as the vibration frequency of a column falls to zero at the buckling load.
  • Scene 6A vocabulary: rare and frequent tokens. Each row of an embedding table updates only when its token appears. When the rarest and the most frequent rows are stable, every row is stable and the slowest row sits at one of the two ends (Theorem 2); no single row beats plain SGD per step (Theorem 3), and momentum helps the vocabulary only above the batch size B× (Corollary 5, Theorem 6).

Scene 2 of 6 · The time step

Without noise, momentum SGD is a mass on a spring computed in steps

Below ηλ = 2(1 + β) the computed swings die out; above it they grow without bound.

Take one direction of the loss, with curvature λ, and no minibatch noise. The heavy-ball update is then wt+1 − wt = β(wt − wt−1) − ηλ wt: the velocity keeps a fraction β of itself, and the spring pulls back by ηλ times the position. Engineers compute how a structure moves in an earthquake or a crash with the same kind of scheme, one time step after another. Both blow up for the same reason: the step is too large for the stiffness. The two pictures below run the same numbers.

How this works: the limit without noise

Each step multiplies the swing by a factor ρ, the larger root in absolute value of z² − (1 + β − ηλ)z + β = 0. The swing shrinks when ρ < 1, which happens exactly when

0 < ηλ < 2(1 + β)

With β → 1 the damping 1 − β vanishes and the update becomes the central-difference scheme, wt+1 − 2wt + wt−1 = −(ωΔt)² wt, with ηλ = (ωΔt)². Its stability limit ωΔt < 2 is the textbook critical time step of explicit dynamics. Momentum below 1 is a damped version of the same scheme, with damping 1 − β, and the limit on ηλ = (ωΔt)² moves from 4 to 2(1 + β).

A real loss has many directions. Without noise they do not interact, and the stiffest one, λ₁, sets the limit 2(1 + β)/λ₁. This is the number the first scene compares η* with. Scenes 3 and 4 show why the true η* can be far smaller.

Scene 3 of 6 · The shaking cable

A stiffness that changes at every step lowers the limit

With random curvature the limit drops from 2(1 + β)/λ to η*1D, and above η*1D the mean square grows even while many cables still look calm.

With a minibatch, the curvature itself is random. Each step sees λt, an average over B random examples, whose mean is λ. On the bridge below, the left half is closed to traffic and its stay cables keep a constant tension. The right half carries traffic, and here each passing load changes the tension of every cable at every step. Both halves have the same average tension, and both are shaken by the same kind of wind gusts (the gradient noise). Each cable is one independent training run of a one-weight model.

How this works: where the lower limit comes from

For one direction with Gaussian features, Theorem 1(a) with a single eigenvalue gives the threshold in closed form. It is the one-dimensional threshold of Corollary 7 of the note, the threshold with momentum of Andreyev et al. (2026, eq. (10)):

η*1D = (2(1 + β)/λ) / (1 + κβ ν²/λ²),   κβ = (1 + β)/(1 − β),   ν²/λ² = 2/B

ν² is the variance of the minibatch curvature λt. Momentum multiplies its effect by κβ, which is 19 at β = 0.9. With β = 0.9 and B = 4 the steady half is stable up to —, the traffic half only up to η*1D = —.

Try 1.4 η*1D. How many of the 200 traffic cables fail within the 1500 steps depends on β and B, and varies from run to run; the counts below are ranges over repeated runs. At β = 0.9 and B = 4 most of them stay calm: about 5 to 20 fail, yet the exact mean of w² grows without bound. With B = 1 few fail at any β, about 2 to 15. More fail with B = 16 or, at B = 4, with smaller β: about 25 to 50 at β = 0.9, B = 16; about 60 to 95 at β = 0.5 and 90 to 125 at β = 0, with B = 4; and at β = 0 or 0.5 with B = 16, 1.4 η*1D is above the limit without curvature noise, 2(1 + β)/λ, so all 200 fail. Where few fail, the mean square is carried by rare, very large swings. Mean-square stability is a statement about that average, and the theorem is exact for it. A single run can look safe above η*1D.

On a real bridge, a cable whose tension varies in time can be driven to large swings below its static limits (parametric excitation). Here the tension is redrawn independently at every step, as a minibatch redraws the curvature.

Scene 4 of 6 · Coupled modes

Every direction alone is stable, yet together they diverge at a step 144.5 times smaller

With Rademacher features each direction alone is stable up to η*1D = 3.8000; tied together by the minibatch, the directions diverge above η* = 0.0263.

Now take P = 256 directions with H = diag(1, 1/√2, …, 1/√256), β = 0.9 and B = 4, the setting of Table 1 of the note. With Rademacher features, every coordinate of every example is +1 or −1, so the curvature along each eigen-direction is exactly λk at every step: zk² = λk. Each direction on its own is the problem of scene 2 without curvature noise, stable up to 2(1 + β)/λ₁ = 3.8. But the minibatch Hessian also has off-diagonal entries zjzk/B, which pass energy from one direction to another at random.

Scene 5 of 6 · The settling rate

Below η* the loss settles at a predicted rate, and the rate falls to zero at η*

The gap ℓ∞ − ℓt shrinks by a factor ρ = 1/z* per step, where z* is the smallest positive root of zK̂(z) = 1, and 1 − ρ falls linearly to zero as η approaches η*.

The model of scene 1 again: P = 32 Gaussian features, H = diag(1, 1/2, …, 1/32), unit label noise, runs started from w = 0. Each of 128 runs has a twin that started training earlier. From step 0 on, run and twin draw the same minibatches and the same label noise, so their difference feels the random curvature but no label noise, and the mean of its loss is the gap ℓ∞ − ℓt of Theorem 1(d), up to the part the twin has not closed yet (below 0.3 %). Read directly from one group of 128 runs started at w = 0, the gap is lost in the run-to-run scatter once it falls below about 5 to 10 % of ℓ∞ (measured at β = 0.9, B = 4, 0.7 η*); the difference with the twin follows it over four decades.

How this works: the rate from the renewal equation

The kernel Kτ of the renewal equation of scene 1 has a rational generating function (Section 1 of the note). With ak = 1 + β − ηλk and ck = p(1 + κ)λk²,

ψ̂k(z) = η²(1 + βz) / ((1 − βz)(1 − (ak² − 2β)z + β²z²)),   K̂(z) = p Σk λk² ψ̂k / (1 − ckzψ̂k)

K̂(1) = S(η), so at η* the root of zK̂(z) = 1 is z = 1. Below η* it moves above 1, and ℓ∞ − ℓt = Cρt + O(r−t) with ρ = 1/z*, r > z* and an explicit C > 0 (Theorem 1(c), (d); (d) needs κk ≥ 1/γ − 2 for every mode, which Gaussian features meet and Rademacher features never do). The page finds z* by bisection between 0 and the first pole of K̂, and checks it against ρ obtained by iterating the exact second-moment recursion, which does not use z*. As η → η*, 1 − ρ ≈ [S′(η*)/Στ(τ + 1)Kτ](η* − η) (Theorem 1(e)); the denominator is the mean renewal time of the kernel.

Where this comes from. For Gaussian features with momentum and batch, Velikanov, Kuznedelev and Yarotsky (ICLR 2023) write the loss generating function in closed form and obtain the rate from rŨ(r) = 1, with a prefactor in the divergent regime; for isotropic gated features the per-step spectrum is a cubic (Everett and Paquette, 2026, Lemma H.4); without momentum and for Gaussian features the exact loss is in Bordelon and Pehlevan (2022, Theorem 1); the LMS precursors are Feuer and Weinstein (1985) and Roy and Shynk (1990). The note's own part is a proof that ρ(𝒯) = 1/z* at finite P for features with mode-dependent kurtosis and a random scale, and, on the stable side, the prefactor C > 0 and the slope of critical slowing down.

A loaded column gives the same warning in its vibrations. The squared lowest natural frequency of a pinned bar falls linearly with the axial load, ω² = ω₀²(1 − P/Pcr), and reaches zero at Pcr; engineers extrapolate measured frequencies to zero to estimate Pcr without buckling the bar (the vibration correlation technique). Here 1 − ρ plays the part of ω²: near η* it falls along a straight line that reaches zero at η*.

The crosses scatter for two reasons, both measured on this model. Sampling: at β = 0.9, B = 4, in three repeated sweeps with other seeds, the fitted 1 − ρ was 0.94 to 1.04 times the prediction at 0.6 η*, 0.77 to 1.18 times at 0.7 and 0.8 η*, 1.11 to 1.40 times at 0.9 η* and 1.31 to 1.83 times at 0.95 η*; near η* the mean is carried by a few runs with large swings, which 128 runs sample poorly (as in scene 3). Transient: with β = 0, and with β = 0.5 at 0.6 η*, the faster term O(r−t) is still present inside the fit window, and the exact mean fitted over the same steps gives up to 1.45 times the asymptotic 1 − ρ (β = 0, B = 16, 0.6 η*); the dotted curve shows it, and the score table gives this number for the current run. The exact second-moment recursion, iterated to convergence, gives the predicted ρ to within 4 × 10−12 for every β, B and slider position.

Scene 6 of 6 · A vocabulary: rare and frequent tokens

In a vocabulary, the slowest row sits at an end, and a single row never beats plain SGD

When the rarest and the most frequent token rows are mean-square stable, every row between them is stable and maxj ρ(qj) = max(ρ(qmin), ρ(qmax)) (Theorem 2). For each row, ρ(q) ≥ (d + 2 − q)/(d + 2 − q + Bq) at every (η, β) (Theorem 3).

In the vocabulary model of Everett and Paquette (2026, Section H.3), each example draws a token j with probability qj and a Gaussian vector in ℝd; row j of the embedding table updates only when token j appears, while one pair (η, β) is shared by all rows. By their Lemma H.12, row j has the marginal law of a single d-dimensional Gaussian feature gated with probability qj, and the linear part of its second-moment recursion is the 3 × 3 matrix M(qj) of Section 2 of the note. How this works: M(q), extreme rows and the speed limit

By Lemma H.12 of Everett and Paquette (2026), row j of the vocabulary has the marginal law of Theorem 1 with H = qjId, γ = 1/qj and κk = 0. On the traced moments (E‖wt‖², E⟨wt, wt−1⟩, E‖wt−1‖²) the linear recursion is the 3 × 3 matrix of Section 2 of the note, with a = 1 + β − ηq and m₁₁ = a² + η²q(d + 2 − q)/B:

M(q) = [[a² + η²q(d + 2 − q)/B, −2aβ, β²], [a, −β, 0], [1, 0, 0]],   det(λI − M(q)) = λ³ + (β − m₁₁)λ² + β(2a² − m₁₁ − β)λ − β³

The page computes ρ(q) = ρ(M(q)) directly from the roots of this cubic. Near β = 1 and small ηq, M(q) approaches a 3 × 3 Jordan block with a triple eigenvalue at 1, where floating-point rounding perturbs numerical eigenvalues by O(εmach1/3) while 1 − ρ is O(1 − β); the slider therefore clamps β ≤ 0.99. Theorem 2 proves that {q ∈ (0, 1] : ρ(q) ≤ s} is an interval for every s < 1, so when qmin and qmax are stable, every row between them is stable and maxj ρ(qj) = max(ρ(qmin), ρ(qmax)). Stability of both ends is needed: at d = B = 1, η = 0.36878, β = 0.51037, ρ(q) is 1.000195, 1.016659, 0.932660 at q = 0.001, 0.2124, 1. Over 2163 random stable settings (d ≤ 200, B ≤ 256, 10−3 ≤ η ≤ 10, q ∈ [10−4, 1]) direct evaluation found zero interior maxima, and M(q) agrees with exact enumeration of 𝒯 to 2 × 10−14.

For a single row of frequency q, write D = d + 2 − q. Theorem 3 gives the exact fixed-β minimum ρ(M(q)) ≥ 1/zβ, attained at ηβ:

zβ = 2(D + Bq) / (D(1 + β²) + √(D²(1 − β²)² + 4β²B²q²)),   ηβ = B(1 − β²zβ²) / (zβ(1 + β)D)

At β = 0 this reduces to 1/z₀ = D/(D + Bq) = (d + 2 − q)/(d + 2 − q + Bq) at η₀ = B/(Bq + D), and for every β > 0, 1/zβ > D/(D + Bq) strictly: momentum never makes a single gated feature contract faster per step than best SGD. Minimising B/ln zvocβ(B) over B gives Eβ = 1/ln zβ(qmin, 1), strictly increasing in β, so Emom = ESGD (Corollary 4; cf. Morwani et al., 2026, for dense Gaussian regression).

Across the whole vocabulary there is a sharp batch crossover B× = (d + 2)/(qmax − qmin). When B − 1 ≤ B×, the rare-row step η₀(qmin) keeps qmax faster than qmin, so tuned momentum is β = 0 and Smom(B) = SSGD(B) (Corollary 5). When B − 1 > B×, large SGD steps make qmax overshoot; tuned β > 0 balances ρ(qmin) = ρ(qmax) at η = 1/ubal and beats SGD, approaching the Polyak limit Smom as Smom(B) = Smom + CmomB−1/2 + O(B−1) (Theorem 6; at d = 8, qmax = 10/11, qmin = 1/11, Cmom = 6.6601, and √B(Smom(B) − Smom) is 7.0126, 6.7686, 6.6941, 6.6708 at B = 10⁴, 10⁵, 10⁶, 10⁷).

The gauge readings carry ±0.006 mm of noise from a fixed seed, so a given bar always gives the same readings; the training runs use fixed seeds too. Theorem, corollary, lemma and table numbers refer to the short note Minibatch Heavy-Ball SGD at Finite Dimension: Exact Threshold, Rate and Critical Slowing Down. Its own parts: the model with coordinate-dependent fourth moments and a random scale; the finite-P rate ρ(𝒯) = 1/z*, the single-exponential prefactor C > 0 on the stable side and the slope of critical slowing down (Theorem 1); in the vocabulary model, that the slowest row is the most or the least frequent one when those two are stable (Theorem 2), the exact single-feature speed limit ρ ≥ (d + 2 − q)/(d + 2 − q + Bq) and fixed-β minimum 1/zβ at ηβ (Theorem 3), the closed form of Eβ and Emom = ESGD (Corollary 4), that below B× momentum takes as many steps as SGD with tuned β = 0 (Corollary 5), and the exact curve Sβ(B), tuned β > 0 above B× and Smom(B) − Smom = CmomB−1/2 + O(B−1) (Theorem 6); and explicit bounds on the distance to the high-rank EMA threshold 2B/(γ tr H) (Corollary 7).
