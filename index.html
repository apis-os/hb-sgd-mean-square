Theorem 5 of the note

Heavy-ball SGD on a quadratic with random minibatch Hessians HBt (mean H) and gradient noise ξt:

wt+1 = ((1 + β)I − ηHBt) wt − β wt−1 − η ξt

When each minibatch averages B random features z = H1/2ω with independent coordinates of mean 0, variance 1 and fourth moment 3 + κ, the second moment E‖wt‖² stays bounded exactly when η is below η*, the smallest positive root (below the first pole) of

S(η) = (ηp / (2(1 − β))) · Σk λk / (1 − ηλkα / (2(1 + β))) = 1

Here λk are the eigenvalues of H, p = 1/B and α = 1 + p(1 + κ)(1 + β)/(1 − β). For η ≥ η* the second moment grows at least linearly in t (Lemma 2, with gradient noise Σξ ≻ 0). For arbitrary per-sample Hessians, η* is the reciprocal of the top eigenvalue of an explicit operator (Theorem 3).

Why it happens

A column and a network with the same amplification curve

Below η* the average loss settles at f/(1 − S(η)) (Proposition 6), and the factor 1/(1 − S(η)) grows without bound as S(η) approaches 1. A pinned steel bar has the same factor: with a small initial bow a₀ and a load P, it bows by a₀/(1 − P/Pcr). Below, the two systems side by side.

One curve for both

Column: the total bow at mid-height is a₀ / (1 − P/Pcr), so x = P/Pcr. Network: once training settles, the average loss is f / (1 − S(η)), where S(η) is the left-hand side of the note's secular equation and f stays finite at the limit (a consequence of Proposition 6), so x = S(η). The height of the network point is measured: the average loss of the 48 runs divided by f. Its position x = S(η) is computed from the formula. Above η* there is no steady level, and the point is drawn at the limit. In a real lab you would not know Pcr; this page knows it so that you can check the method below.

Load P
The force pushing along the bar, in newtons. 1000 N is roughly the weight of 100 kg.
Buckling, Pcr
Past a certain load the bar bows sideways and stops carrying more. That load is the critical load Pcr.
Pinned ends
Supports that hold the ends of the bar in place but let them rotate, like a hinge.
Hydraulic jack and load cell
The jack is a hand-pumped piston that pushes the bar from below. The load cell is a force sensor at the top; the digital readout shows its value.
Dial gauge
A clock-like instrument for small movements. One turn of the long needle is 1 mm and each mark is 0.01 mm; the small dial counts turns.
Initial imperfection a₀
No real bar is perfectly straight. a₀ is its small initial bow at mid-height, here around 1 mm over 1 m.

Terms on the network side

Step size η
How far each update moves the weights along the negative gradient (the learning rate).
Minibatch, B
Each update uses B = 4 random examples (the batch size chosen above), so both the gradient and the curvature it sees are noisy.
Momentum β
Each update keeps a fraction β of the previous one, like a mass that carries inertia.
Loss ℓ
Here ℓt = tr(H E[wt wt⊤]), twice the expected excess loss, where wt is the distance of the weights from the best fit.
Threshold η*
The step size above which ℓ grows without bound (mean-square instability). In this model it is the smallest positive root of the secular equation, Theorem 5 of the note.

Why the column warns

Beam theory gives the extra sideways deflection that the gauge reads at mid-height of a pinned bar with initial bow a₀:

δ = a₀ · (P/Pcr) / (1 − P/Pcr)

At half the critical load the extra deflection equals the initial bow. At 90 % it is nine times larger. At the critical load the denominator vanishes and the bar buckles. Euler's formula Pcr = π²EI/L² gives Pcr for an ideal bar, but a 0.1 mm error in a 6 mm thickness changes I by 5 %, and real pins are never ideal. So engineers prefer to measure the bar they have.

Southwell divided the same relation by P:

δ/P = δ/Pcr + a₀/Pcr

This is a straight line in the plane (δ, δ/P). Its slope is 1/Pcr and its intercept gives a₀. Readings between 50 % and 75 % of the critical load are enough, and the bar survives the test. The method is still used for columns, plates and shells.

The same warning in training

The network side runs heavy-ball SGD on a quadratic loss with random minibatch Hessians,

wt+1 = ((1 + β)I − ηHBt) wt − β wt−1 − η ξt

with H = diag(1, 1/2, …, 1/32), Gaussian features, β = 0.9, B = 4 and unit label noise. For this random-feature model the note shows that the expected loss obeys an exact convolution equation (Proposition 6). When ηλk · max(α, 2 − α) < 2(1 + β) for every k, its kernel sums to

S(η) = (ηp / (2(1 − β))) · Σk λk / (1 − ηλkα / (2(1 + β))),   α = 1 + p(1 + β)(1 + κ)/(1 − β)

with p = 1/B and κ = 0 for Gaussian features. η* is the smallest positive root of S(η) = 1, the one below the first pole. Below η* and inside that condition, the equation implies the steady loss f/(1 − S(η)); above η* there is none. For these settings η* = —. Without curvature noise (every minibatch seeing the Hessian H itself) the limit would be 2(1 + β)/λ₁ = —, — times larger; the gap is the effect of the random curvature.

Where the analogy stops

The column is a static problem: the load rises slowly and the bar finds a new equilibrium at each step. Training is dynamic and random. The weights never stop moving, and what grows without bound is the mean square of their fluctuations. Scene 3 draws a new stiffness at every step.

S(η) is not proportional to η in the way P/Pcr is proportional to P, so a Southwell line drawn in η is only an approximation near the limit.

The four scenes

  • Scene 1The theorem applied, and why training behaves like a buckling column. Above.
  • Scene 2The time step. The mass, spring and damper that an engineer integrates in time. With too large a time step the computation blows up; that is the noiseless limit 2(1 + β)/λ.
  • Scene 3The shaking cable. A stiffness that varies at random makes a structure unstable below the limit computed without noise. This is the minibatch effect on one direction.
  • Scene 4Coupled modes. Each direction alone is stable, yet the coupling lowers the limit. With Rademacher features, P = 256, β = 0.9 and B = 4, the limit is 144.5 times smaller than the one-direction limit.

Scene 2 of 4 · The time step

Without noise, momentum SGD is a mass on a spring computed in steps

Below ηλ = 2(1 + β) the computed swings die out; above it they grow without bound.

Take one direction of the loss, with curvature λ, and no minibatch noise. The heavy-ball update is then wt+1 − wt = β(wt − wt−1) − ηλ wt: the velocity keeps a fraction β of itself, and the spring pulls back by ηλ times the position. Engineers compute how a structure moves in an earthquake or a crash with the same kind of scheme, one time step after another. Both blow up for the same reason: the step is too large for the stiffness. The two pictures below run the same numbers.

How this works: the limit without noise

Each step multiplies the swing by a factor ρ, the larger root in absolute value of z² − (1 + β − ηλ)z + β = 0. The swing shrinks when ρ < 1, which happens exactly when

0 < ηλ < 2(1 + β)

With β → 1 the damping 1 − β vanishes and the update becomes the central-difference scheme, wt+1 − 2wt + wt−1 = −(ωΔt)² wt, with ηλ = (ωΔt)². Its stability limit ωΔt < 2 is the textbook critical time step of explicit dynamics. Momentum below 1 is a damped version of the same scheme, with damping 1 − β, and the limit on ηλ = (ωΔt)² moves from 4 to 2(1 + β).

A real loss has many directions. Without noise they do not interact, and the stiffest one, λ₁, sets the limit 2(1 + β)/λ₁. This is the number the first scene compares η* with. Scenes 3 and 4 show why the true η* can be far smaller.

Scene 3 of 4 · The shaking cable

A stiffness that changes at every step lowers the limit

With random curvature the limit drops from 2(1 + β)/λ to η*1D, and above η*1D the mean square grows even while many cables still look calm.

With a minibatch, the curvature itself is random. Each step sees λt, an average over B random examples, whose mean is λ. On the bridge below, the left half is closed to traffic and its stay cables keep a constant tension. The right half carries traffic, and here each passing load changes the tension of every cable at every step. Both halves have the same average tension, and both are shaken by the same kind of wind gusts (the gradient noise). Each cable is one independent training run of a one-weight model.

How this works: where the lower limit comes from

For one direction with Gaussian features, Theorem 5 with a single eigenvalue gives the threshold in closed form. It is the one-dimensional threshold of Section 4 of the note:

η*1D = (2(1 + β)/λ) / (1 + κβ ν²/λ²),   κβ = (1 + β)/(1 − β),   ν²/λ² = 2/B

ν² is the variance of the minibatch curvature λt. Momentum multiplies its effect by κβ, which is 19 at β = 0.9. With β = 0.9 and B = 4 the steady half is stable up to —, the traffic half only up to η*1D = —.

Try 1.4 η*1D. How many of the 200 traffic cables fail within the 1500 steps depends on β and B, and varies from run to run; the counts below are ranges over repeated runs. At β = 0.9 and B = 4 most of them stay calm: about 5 to 20 fail, yet the exact mean of w² grows without bound. With B = 1 few fail at any β, about 2 to 15. More fail with B = 16 or, at B = 4, with smaller β: about 25 to 50 at β = 0.9, B = 16; about 60 to 95 at β = 0.5 and 90 to 125 at β = 0, with B = 4; and at β = 0 or 0.5 with B = 16, 1.4 η*1D is above the limit without curvature noise, 2(1 + β)/λ, so all 200 fail. Where few fail, the mean square is carried by rare, very large swings. Mean-square stability is a statement about that average, and the theorem is exact for it. A single run can look safe above η*1D.

On a real bridge, a cable whose tension varies in time can be driven to large swings below its static limits (parametric excitation). Here the tension is redrawn independently at every step, as a minibatch redraws the curvature.

Scene 4 of 4 · Coupled modes

Every direction alone is stable, yet together they diverge at a step 144.5 times smaller

With Rademacher features each direction alone is stable up to η*1D = 3.8000; tied together by the minibatch, the directions diverge above η* = 0.0263.

Now take P = 256 directions with H = diag(1, 1/√2, …, 1/√256), β = 0.9 and B = 4, the setting of Table 1 of the note. With Rademacher features, every coordinate of every example is +1 or −1, so the curvature along each eigen-direction is exactly λk at every step: zk² = λk. Each direction on its own is the problem of scene 2 without curvature noise, stable up to 2(1 + β)/λ₁ = 3.8. But the minibatch Hessian also has off-diagonal entries zjzk/B, which pass energy from one direction to another at random.
